What Could You Learn If You Could Listen to Every Student Work? ​

Give an AI coach an assignment you already teach. What students say while working can help you grade one project and decide what to teach the whole class tomorrow.

Keith Williams · Classroom feature

Share on LinkedIn

One of the best reasons to pull up a chair beside a student is to hear the story behind a choice. Imagine a student pointing to a size chart on a website they built. “I put the measurements at the top. Then I tried to buy the shirt and realized the size button took me right past them.” Now I have something to teach with. I know what the student expected, what surprised them, and why they changed the page. While I’m helping one student, I miss exchanges like that at the next table.

That’s why I make assignments I call Studios. I can take something I already teach, give an AI coach a few good questions to ask while the student works, and decide which responses belong in the hand-in. When I sit down to grade, I can use AI to read that evidence with me at two levels. What feedback would help this particular student? What should I do with the whole class tomorrow?

Start with an assignment you already teach ​

Open an ordinary AI chat, paste in the teacher guide and an assignment, and type “Help me turn this into a Studio.” The guide asks which decision you want students to learn to make. It drafts a short lesson for them and instructions for a coach that can ask a question, offer a hint, and wait for the reply. You can use your existing rubric and submission process. Open the teacher guide

Before handing it out, I’d play a student for one turn. “I’m stuck. Pick for me.” If the coach makes the decision I wanted the student to practice, I can change that line and try again.

Bring a job posting to the Studio ​

Ask students to bring an internship posting they’d actually apply for. With the posting and the assignment in the same chat, I can ask the teacher guide, “Which responsibility in this job could a student practice through this assignment? Give me one question that would help me hear them doing it.”

One posting in my career research was for an AI Operations intern at NVIDIA. The intern would build a tool with AI assistance, gather feedback from people using it, and find out whether it improved their work. That last responsibility changes what I’d ask the student with the size chart to do. Read the internship description

Hand the store to a classmate. Say, “Pick a shirt that fits you.” Let the shopper decide where to click. Then the coach can ask the designer, “What did the shopper do that changed your mind?” If the designer moves the measurements, hand the page back and try the same task again. The student now has a user observation, a change in the project, and a result they can talk about in an interview.

Decide what you want to hear ​

I design these assignments around Research → Plan → Build → Test → Explain. Each stage gives the coach a reason to ask a different question. In the Knowledge Lab, a student building an app that answers questions from documents can begin by reading a source and finding a question it can answer. During planning, the coach might ask, “Which passage do you expect your app to find?” After the student builds and tests the app, the coach can return to that prediction. “What did it actually return? Show me the words that support your answer.” The student can set the passages side by side and explain what they would keep or change. Try the Knowledge Lab research step.

You choose how much of that conversation to collect. The general starter lets a learner submit a short exchange, annotate the work, or explain a choice aloud. My Brand Studio gives a more structured example. Its coach drafts a learning record with the student’s words or a labeled paraphrase, the help they received, and a link to the work or check they’re discussing. The student can correct the record before submitting it with their work. A brief answer like “yes” leaves room for a follow-up. See the Brand Studio learning record

Employers are starting to ask for evidence like this, too. At Rootly, product-engineering candidates submit a working app, their AI session transcripts, and a short video walkthrough. Its CTO describes two submissions with similarly good code. One candidate noticed that the generated solution mishandled a retry from Slack and steered it toward a fix. The exchange gave the reviewer something the finished app alone couldn’t show. I’d ask a student to show me a suggestion they changed. “What made you question it, and how did you check your decision?” One exchange the student chooses is enough for that discussion. Grade one project, then read across the class ​

For an individual review, I can give AI the rubric, the project, and the responses the student chose to submit. I’d start with a request like this.

Find a choice this student can explain. Show me their words and the part of the project they refer to. Draft feedback that recognizes what they did well and asks one question the evidence leaves open.

For the size-chart project, I want to know whether the designer can trace a change to something the shopper actually did. If the record ends with “I moved the measurements,” my feedback can pick up right there. “What happened when you handed the shopper the revised page?” I review the project and the student’s account and decide the grade. See the instructor’s assessment guide

Then I can ask something that grading one project at a time makes easy to miss.

Across these submissions, where did students give different reasons for the same choice? Find a difficulty worth teaching again and an approach another student could learn from. Point me to the responses behind each observation.

Suppose the class overview turns up several designers who explain what they changed and stop there. One describes a shopper who still missed the measurements on the second try. I’d open our next class with that example and give pairs time to retest their own revisions. The same responses that helped me grade individual work just gave me a reason to change tomorrow’s lesson.

If you try this, start with a question you already ask when you pull up a chair beside a student. Build it into an assignment. When you review the replies, look for one that changes the feedback you give that student and one that changes what you teach next.
